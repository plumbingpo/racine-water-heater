How Franklin Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year if their water is hard.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular service, a standard tank usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like discolored hot water, rumbling, or a damp spot under the tank, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule costs less and is far less stressful than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Take a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank steel, and suddenly a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a flooded utility room.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Water heaters in and around Franklin face a familiar set of problems. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. Once the protective anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel starts to rust from the inside, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
